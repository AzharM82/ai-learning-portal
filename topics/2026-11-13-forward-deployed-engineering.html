Forward deployed engineering: discovery, prototyping, and landing value

A capable model or platform does not deploy itself. Between what the product does and what a customer actually needs sits messy data, legacy systems, security reviews, and users who cannot describe the workflow until they see it working. A forward deployed engineer (FDE) closes that gap from inside the customer: find the real problem, prove it on real data in days, harden it into production, and stay until people actually use it.

02 The one-idea version

The FDE sells an outcome, not a product or a service. A platform engineer (Palantir’s “Dev”) builds one capability for many customers; a forward deployed engineer (“Delta”) applies many capabilities for one customer — and is measured by that customer’s production adoption and workflow impact, not by features shipped. The loop is discover → prototype → harden → land, then pave the gravel road so the next customer starts further ahead.

03 The picture: the deployment loop

One customer, many capabilities — and every loop feeds what was learned back into the platform.

04 How it works

Six mechanics: discover before you build, wedge small, prototype on real data, evaluate before hardening, land adoption, and pave what you learned.

1

Discover in the workflow, not the meeting room

Sit with the people who do the work. Watch the current process end to end, note where time, re-typing, and workarounds live, and separate the requested feature from the real problem. OpenAI’s FDE description puts discovery and technical scoping first for a reason: the customer often cannot articulate the need until an engineer sees the workflow. Write the problem in the customer’s words — who hurts, how often, at what cost — and name the metric and its baseline before any code.

2

Scope the smallest high-leverage wedge

Pick one workflow, one user group, and one outcome you can move in weeks — not the whole transformation. Define success criteria, non-goals, the sponsor who can unblock you, and a kill criterion if the data or access never arrives. Sequence delivery explicitly and remove blockers early (data access, security review, identity) because those, not modelling, are what usually stall enterprise AI. A narrow wedge that lands beats a broad plan that demos.

3

Prototype on real data, in days, in front of real users

Build the smallest end-to-end slice against the customer’s real (or faithfully sanitized) data and systems, and demo it to the actual users this week. A slide is not a prototype and synthetic data proves almost nothing: the failures that matter — messy fields, permissions, edge cases — only appear on real data. Throwaway code is acceptable here; the prototype’s job is to test whether the value is real, fast enough that a wrong direction costs days, not a quarter.

4

Evaluate before you harden

Turn the prototype’s real cases into an eval set with expected outcomes, graded with users or domain experts. OpenAI measures FDE work through eval-driven feedback: the eval tells you whether a change helped, gives the customer a reason to trust the system, and gives product and research a precise account of where the model succeeds and fails. Do not productionize a demo nobody has graded — harden only what the eval says is good enough for the stated risk.

5

Harden inside the customer’s boundary

Now do the unglamorous engineering: integrate with their identity, data, and existing tools; pass their security and compliance review; add observability, retries, and a runbook; and deploy where their sensitive data is allowed to live. Contribute production-grade code, not a notebook hand-off. Make trade-offs between scope, speed, and quality explicitly with the customer, and protect the delivery date by cutting scope — never by silently cutting the safety or eval gates.

6

Land it, then pave the road behind you

Go-live is the midpoint. Train and pair with the customer’s own engineers, watch adoption signals, fix the friction users actually hit, and measure the workflow impact against the baseline from step 1. Then do the second half of the job: codify working patterns into tools, playbooks, and platform features, and carry field feedback back to product — Palantir notes some of its most valuable product additions originated in the field this way. Leave the customer able to run and extend the system without you.

05 Code sketch

An engagement as code: no prototype without a baseline, no production without an eval gate.

from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass
class Engagement:
    problem: str            # in the customer's words, not a feature request
    metric: str             # e.g. "minutes per claim summary"
    baseline: float | None = None
    eval_cases: list = field(default_factory=list)  # real cases, graded
    wedge: str = ""         # one workflow, one user group

    def can_prototype(self) -> bool:
        return self.baseline is not None and bool(self.wedge)

    def can_ship(self, eval_score: float, threshold: float = 0.9) -> bool:
        # Eval gate first, then adoption is measured against baseline.
        return len(self.eval_cases) >= 20 and eval_score >= threshold

def land(eng: Engagement, before: float, after: float) -> dict:
    assert eng.can_prototype(), "discovery incomplete: no baseline / wedge"
    impact = (before - after) / before
    return {"impact_pct": round(100 * impact, 1),
            "landed": impact > 0,  # value, not go-live, is the verdict
            "pave": "codify the pattern for the platform team"}

06 Failure modes

Five ways an FDE engagement fails — each with a one-line fix.

1

Building the requested featureFix: shadow the work first and re-state the problem, metric, and baseline in the customer’s words before scoping anything.

The sponsor asks for a chatbot, a dashboard, or “AI for this team,” and the engineer builds exactly that — only to discover the real cost was data entry between two systems nobody mentioned. Discovery skipped is scope wasted.

2

Slideware and synthetic-data demosFix: get a real, sanitized data sample in week one and demo the smallest end-to-end slice to actual users.

The POC shines in a steering meeting and dies on contact with production data, permissions, and edge cases. Everyone agreed to a fiction, and the rework arrives after the credibility is spent.

3

Prototype debt shipped as productionFix: label prototype code as throwaway, pass the eval gate, then deliberately harden — security, observability, runbook — before rollout.

The two-week demo quietly becomes the system of record: no evals, no monitoring, no owner. It works until it doesn’t, in front of the customer who trusted you most.

4

Consulting drift: everything bespoke, nothing generalizesFix: build on shared platform primitives and log every one-off; if a pattern repeats twice, propose it as a platform feature or playbook.

Each engagement becomes custom services work. Margins sink, the product learns nothing, and the FDE team turns into a consultancy with better branding — exactly the trap Kevin Bai’s 101 warns against.

5

Go-live mistaken for doneFix: define landing as adoption + measured impact + a customer team that can run it alone, and stay until all three are true.

The system deploys, the launch email goes out, usage flatlines, and the workflow quietly reverts to spreadsheets. The FDE has left, so nobody owns the adoption problem — and the renewal pays for it.

08 Sample prompts

Copy one into your AI assistant and apply it to your own work.

RUN DISCOVERY FOR AN ENGAGEMENT

Act as a forward deployed engineer starting discovery at this customer: [describe the company, the team, and what they asked for]. Do not propose a solution yet. Give me: (1) the questions I should ask while shadowing users, grouped by workflow, data, systems, and constraints, (2) how to separate the requested feature from the real problem, with the re-statement I should test with the sponsor, (3) the metric and baseline I should capture before building, and how to measure the baseline honestly, (4) the access and blockers to surface in week one — data, security, identity, procurement — and (5) a one-page discovery summary template I can fill in and get signed off.

SCOPE A TWO-WEEK PROTOTYPE

Scope a two-week forward deployed prototype from this discovery summary: [paste it]. Give me: (1) the wedge — one workflow, one user group, one measurable outcome — and what is explicitly out of scope, (2) a day-by-day plan that gets real (or faithfully sanitized) customer data flowing by day two and a demo in front of actual users by the end of week one, (3) the eval set: 20+ real cases with expected outcomes and who grades them, plus the pass threshold that would justify hardening, (4) the risks that would kill the prototype (access, data quality, security review) and the fallback for each, and (5) the demo script: what I show, in what order, and the decision I ask for at the end.

LAND IT AND PAVE THE ROAD

This prototype worked and the customer wants it in production: [describe the prototype, the users, and the customer’s environment]. Give me a landing plan with: (1) the hardening checklist — integrations, identity and permissions, security/compliance review, observability, runbook, and ownership — in dependency order, (2) the rollout and adoption plan: pilot group, training, the friction signals to watch, and how impact is measured against the discovery baseline, (3) the handover: what the customer’s own engineers must be able to do before I step back, and how I verify it, and (4) the pave-the-road memo to my product team: which parts generalize, which stay bespoke and why, and the platform feature or playbook I am proposing.

10 Flashcards

Click a card (or focus it and press Enter / Space) to flip it.

Forward deployed engineer (FDE)
A software engineer employed by the vendor who embeds with one customer to make the product deliver a real outcome there: discovery, prototyping, integration, production rollout, and adoption — writing production code throughout, not advising from the side.
Dev vs Delta
Palantir’s split: a Dev builds one capability for many customers on the core platform; a Delta (the FDE) applies many capabilities for one customer. Same engineering bar, opposite direction of generalization.
Discovery
The phase before building: shadow users in their real workflow, separate the requested feature from the real problem, and capture the metric and its baseline. Discovery decides what the real work is; skipping it scopes the wrong thing beautifully.
The wedge
The smallest first scope that can land: one workflow, one user group, one measurable outcome, in weeks. Land the wedge, earn trust and access, then expand — land-and-expand beats the big-bang transformation that never ships.
Prototype on real data
The smallest end-to-end slice, built in days against the customer’s real or faithfully sanitized data and demoed to actual users. Synthetic data and slides hide exactly the failures — mess, permissions, edge cases — that decide the engagement.
Eval gate
A set of real cases with expected outcomes, graded before production. It is the trust mechanism with the customer, the go/no-go for hardening, and the feedback channel that tells product and research where the model actually fails.
Gravel road → paved highway
The FDE builds the rough first path for one customer; core engineering then generalizes it into the platform so the next ten customers get a paved road. If nothing gets paved, the motion has drifted into consulting.
Landing value
The finish line is not go-live: it is production adoption, workflow impact measured against the discovery baseline, and a customer team that can run and extend the system without the FDE.
Consulting drift
The failure mode where every engagement becomes bespoke services work: no shared primitives, nothing codified back, product learns nothing. The guardrails are a real platform to build on and a pave-the-road step in every engagement.
When FDE does not fit
If the product is self-serve, the buyer is technical, and contracts are small, embedding engineers cannot pay for themselves. FDE fits complex products, non-technical buyers, regulated or integrated environments, and deals large enough to carry the cost.

11 Reach for this when…

A capable product keeps stalling inside customers — pilots demo well and never reach production. The gap is data, integration, workflow, and trust, and that gap is engineering work done inside the customer, not more features.
The buyer cannot specify what they need — a complex platform sold to a non-technical organization. Nobody can write the requirements document; an embedded engineer has to discover the problem by watching the work and prototyping against it.
You are entering a new vertical or landing a flagship account — you do not yet know what the product should become there. One landed customer, with its patterns paved back into the platform, teaches more than a year of market research.
