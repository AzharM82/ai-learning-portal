Fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting: how to choose

Three levers, three different jobs. Prompting steers behavior in-session and costs nothing to change. RAG hands the model fresh facts at query time. Fine-tuning bakes behavior, style, and format into the weights — the most powerful lever, and the most expensive to get wrong. The mistake is picking your favorite first; the discipline is climbing the ladder cheapest-first and only fine-tuning where prompting provably falls short.

02 The one-idea version

Ask what changes: if the facts change without a retrain, retrieve them (RAG); if the behavior, format, or tone must stay constant and prompting can't hold it steady, bake it in (fine-tuning); everything else stays in the prompt. Prompt first, RAG second, fine-tune last — and measure each step, because a fine-tune that can't beat a well-built prompt on your evals is an expensive lesson.

04 How it works

Seven mechanics. Each is one concrete move.

1

Climb the ladder cheapest-first: prompt, then RAG, then fine-tune

OpenAI's model-optimization workflow is explicit: establish eval baselines, iterate on prompts first, and fine-tune only for tasks where prompts fall short — consistent formatting, more examples than fit in context, or proprietary data. Each rung is an order of magnitude more expensive to build and change than the last. A fine-tune that can't beat a well-built prompt on your evals is an expensive lesson in skipping steps.

2

Ask what changes: facts go to retrieval, behavior goes to weights

The master question: should the right answer change without a retrain? If yes — prices, docs, policies, weekly manuals — that's RAG, because weights are a bad database: every fact change needs a new training run, and the model can't cite where it "learned" the fact from. If the format, tone, tool-call discipline, or task style must stay constant across sessions, that's fine-tuning territory. Facts move; behavior stays.

3

Apply the data-volatility test

Put a date on the knowledge: hourly → retrieval with aggressive refresh; weekly → RAG with re-indexing on change; never → it can live in a prompt or in weights. Now put a volatility test on the behavior: does the output template drift on long threads, does the tool-call schema break under load, does the tone slip? Prompting buys you one example per few tokens; fine-tuning buys you thousands of examples compressed into weights — that's when more examples than fit in the context window justify the jump.

4

Meet the fine-tuning bar: 50–100+ quality pairs, evals first, held-out validation

Fine-tuning is supervised learning — it teaches the model to imitate your data, including your data's mistakes. Bring 50–100+ high-quality input/output pairs minimum (more for complex tasks), each representing exactly the behavior you want, and validate on examples the training never saw. And never fine-tune without yesterday's discipline: evals before the run, so the fine-tune's gain over the prompt is a measured number, not a vibe. No evals, no fine-tune.

5

Do the cost math — fine-tuning is often about cheaper inference

The underrated reason to fine-tune: a smaller fine-tuned model can match a bigger general model on your narrow task at a fraction of the per-token cost and latency. That's the economic engine — pay once for training, save on every call forever. So track cost per successful task, not per API call: a fine-tune that matches GPT-scale quality on a mini-scale model can cut serving cost 5–10x, which is what makes high-volume features affordable.

6

Combine, don't choose: fine-tune the behavior, RAG the facts, prompt the glue

Production systems stack the levers: a fine-tuned model that reliably cites sources and follows the output schema, retrieving fresh documents at query time, with a prompt that glues the retrieved context to the current task. RAG-vs-fine-tuning is a false rivalry — Microsoft's own Foundry guidance says exactly this: RAG for grounded answers on private or changing data, fine-tuning to change model behavior and task performance. The question is never "which one" — it's "which job does each do?"

7

Re-climb the ladder when base models improve

Base models get smarter every year, and each generation retires some fine-tunes: what required baking into weights last year may work with plain prompting on the new model. Before re-training anything on a model upgrade, re-run your eval suite with prompt-only on the new base model. If the gap closed, you just saved a training run and its maintenance. The ladder is not a one-time climb — it's a per-model review.

05 Code sketch

A decision router in ~25 lines: encode the flowchart as a function you can run on every feature.

def choose_lever(facts_change, behavior_unstable, n_examples, has_evals):
    """facts_change: answers must change without a retrain.
       behavior_unstable: prompting can't hold format/tone/tools steady.
       n_examples: quality input/output pairs in hand. has_evals: suite exists."""
    plan = ["prompt-engineer first"]   # the ladder always starts here
    if facts_change:
        plan.append("add RAG (retrieve fresh facts, cite sources)")
    if behavior_unstable:
        if n_examples >= 50 and has_evals:
            plan.append("fine-tune (bake in behavior), beat prompt on evals")
        else:
            plan.append("BLOCKED: collect 50+ examples + evals first")
    return plan

# support bot over a weekly-changing manual: facts move, format must hold
print(choose_lever(facts_change=True, behavior_unstable=True,
                   n_examples=120, has_evals=True))

#    'fine-tune (bake in behavior), beat prompt on evals']

06 Failure modes

Five ways teams pick the wrong lever — each with the fix.

1

Fine-tuning volatile facts into the weightsFix: facts that change go to retrieval; weights are for behavior that never changes.

Training this week's prices, policies, or manual revisions into the model guarantees confident, stale answers between retrains — and you can't point at a source when it hallucinates. The retrain treadmill is expensive; the retrieval index updates in minutes.

2

Fine-tuning on 12 examples and no evalsFix: 50–100+ quality pairs, evals written first, held-out validation — or stay on the prompt.

Supervised fine-tuning imitates your data, mistakes included. With a dozen examples you're teaching the model your noise, and without evals you can't even tell whether the fine-tune beat the prompt it replaced. This is the most common fine-tuning failure: all of the cost, none of the evidence.

3

Stuffing the whole corpus into the prompt instead of RAGFix: when knowledge exceeds what fits (or what the model reliably attends to), retrieve and cite.

The reverse failure: dumping 50 pages into context "so the model knows everything." Lost-in-the-middle attention means the model reliably reads the start and end and fuzzes the middle — plus you pay for those tokens on every call. RAG retrieves the few pages that matter and lets the model cite them.

4

Fine-tuning what a model upgrade already fixedFix: on every base-model upgrade, re-run evals prompt-only before re-training anything.

Fine-tunes rot as base models improve: the format discipline you baked in last year is often the new model's default behavior today. Teams that re-fine-tune on schedule instead of re-evaluating on schedule pay for training runs that buy nothing.

5

Treating the levers as rivals instead of a stackFix: fine-tune the behavior (citations, schema, tone), RAG the facts, prompt the glue.

"Should we do RAG or fine-tuning?" is the wrong question when the answer is both: a fine-tuned model that reliably cites sources is a better RAG reader, and RAG facts keep the fine-tuned behavior honest. Picking one lever and forbidding the others leaves the easy wins on the table.

LEVER PICKER

I need to choose between prompt engineering, RAG, and fine-tuning for an AI feature. Decide for me. FEATURE: [one sentence — what it does] KNOWLEDGE: [what facts it needs; how often they change: hourly/weekly/rarely/never] BEHAVIOR: [required output format, tone, tool-call schema — and WHERE prompting currently breaks] DATA: [how many quality input/output examples I have] EVALS: [yes/no — what the suite measures] Output: 1. Your recommendation as a ladder: what to do first, second, third. 2. For each lever: exactly what job it does here, and what job it must NOT do. 3. The single riskiest assumption in your recommendation, and how to test it in under a day. Be decisive. No "it depends" without naming what it depends on.

FINE-TUNE READINESS AUDIT

Audit whether my team is ready to fine-tune. Answer each with PASS / FAIL / BORDERLINE and one sentence of evidence. 1. DATASET: I have 50-100+ input/output pairs, each showing exactly the target behavior. [Paste count + 3 samples] 2. LABEL QUALITY: examples were reviewed by someone who knows the task; noisy or auto-generated ones are flagged. 3. EVALS: a suite exists with tasks + graders, and I have a prompt-only baseline score. 4. HELD-OUT: 10-20% of examples are reserved for validation and will never train. 5. THE PROMPT GAP: I can point to specific failures that persist after serious prompt iteration. [Describe them] 6. COST CASE: I've estimated per-task cost of the fine-tuned small model vs the bigger general model. If any item FAILs, tell me the single fastest fix and whether to fine-tune now or wait. No hedging.

RAG-BEFORE-WEIGHTS REVIEW

My team wants to fine-tune a model on our documentation. Challenge the plan before I approve it. THE PLAN: [what we'd fine-tune on, how often the docs change, what failures we're trying to fix] Do the data-volatility test: 1. Which parts of the knowledge change without a retrain? For each: how RAG would serve it instead, including citation. 2. Which parts are actually BEHAVIOR (format, tone, tool discipline)? For those: could prompting or a smaller fine-tune on BEHAVIOR-ONLY examples (no facts) fix it? 3. Rewrite the plan as a stack: fine-tune the behavior, RAG the facts, prompt the glue — or argue, with specifics, why pure fine-tuning still wins. Output the rewritten plan plus the one experiment that would settle it fastest.
