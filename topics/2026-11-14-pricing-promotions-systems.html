Pricing and promotions systems: elasticity, discounting, and guardrails

Every price change is a bet about how demand will move, and every discount is code that spends margin. A pricing system makes both bets explicit: estimate elasticity by segment, encode promotions as bounded objects with floors and expiry, and put automated changes behind guardrails — so you learn what a discount actually bought instead of just watching conversion go up.

02 The one-idea version

A price is a control, not a label — and elasticity tells you what the control does. Elasticity E = %ΔQ / %ΔP: if |E| > 1 demand is elastic (cut price, volume more than compensates; raise it and revenue falls), if |E| < 1 it is inelastic (a rise lifts revenue), and at |E| = 1 revenue is flat. A promotion is just a temporary, targeted price cut — so it obeys the same math, and it only earns its cost if the incremental margin, measured against a holdout, beats giving the discount to people who would have paid full price anyway.

03 The picture: from list price to guarded checkout

Elasticity informs the price; the promotion engine applies bounded discounts; guardrails veto anything unsafe before checkout sees it.

04 How it works

Seven mechanics: measure elasticity honestly, price from it, encode promos as objects, control stacking, experiment, guardrail, and reconcile.

1

Estimate elasticity — by segment, at a price point, with the midpoint method

Compute E = %ΔQ / %ΔP using midpoint (arc) percentages — change divided by the average of the two values — so the estimate is the same whether price rose or fell (OpenStax 5.1). Never quote one global elasticity: elasticity varies along the same demand curve (0.45 in one interval, 1.47 in another in the OpenStax example), by segment, channel, and time horizon. Estimate it from price experiments or clean natural variation, log the price range it was observed in, and refuse to extrapolate far outside that range. Cross-price elasticity (positive = substitutes, negative = complements) tells you when discounting one product just cannibalises another.

2

Let elasticity set the direction, margin set the limit

Use the revenue rule from OpenStax 5.3: elastic → a cut can raise total revenue; inelastic → a rise raises revenue and a cut destroys it; unitary → revenue is unchanged, so decide on margin and strategy instead. Then check profit, not just revenue: the Lerner intuition — optimal markup over marginal cost shrinks as demand gets more elastic — means highly elastic products cannot carry fat margins. Price to the segment’s elasticity and willingness to pay, keep a single auditable list price per SKU/channel, and version every price change with who, why, experiment ID, and effective window.

3

Encode every promotion as a bounded object, never as ad-hoc math

Model the Stripe split: a coupon is the discount logic (percent_off or amount_off, duration, currency), and a promotion code is the customer-facing string that wraps it with distribution controls. Every promo object must carry: eligible products/SKUs, eligible customers or first-time-only flag, minimum basket, start and expiry (redeem_by / expires_at), max_redemptions overall and per customer, and active/inactive state. If a field is missing, the promo does not launch. Deleting or deactivating must stop new redemptions without rewriting history on orders already placed.

4

Decide stacking, ordering, and the floor — before anyone finds the loophole

Write the precedence rules in code: can codes stack with each other, with sale prices, with loyalty points? In what order do percentage and fixed discounts apply, and is tax computed before or after? Cap the combined discount, and enforce a hard floor — final price never below cost + fees + minimum margin, and never negative. Validate eligibility again at checkout (not just when the code was entered), make redemption atomic so a single-use code cannot be spent twice in parallel sessions, and return a clear reason when a code is rejected instead of silently dropping it.

5

Run discounts as experiments with a holdout

Conversion almost always rises when something gets cheaper — that proves nothing. Randomise a holdout that sees no promo (or use geo/time holdouts where user-level is impossible), pre-register the primary metric as incremental contribution margin per eligible visitor, and measure the tail: repeat purchase at full price, returns, support contacts, and pull-forward (did the promo just borrow next month’s sales?). Report cannibalisation explicitly: full-price buyers who used the code are a cost, not a win. Kill or shrink promos whose lift is rented, not created.

6

Put guardrails around every automated price change

Five limits, enforced in the pricing service — not in a wiki: (1) floor from cost, fees, and minimum margin plus any contractual/MAP constraints; (2) ceiling from list/MSRP and fairness/trust limits; (3) speed limit — max % change per day/week and a minimum interval between changes, and never change a price mid-checkout for a customer already paying; (4) approval ladder — auto-approve inside the band, human review near the edges or on key-value items, escalate outside; (5) kill switch — a named owner can freeze all automated changes in minutes when feeds go stale or wrong. Refuse to price from stale inputs: timestamp every signal and fail closed, not to zero.

7

Reconcile, audit, and watch for abuse as a first-class signal

Every order stores list price, each discount applied (code, coupon ID, amount), final price, and the price version — so finance can reconcile promo spend to the cent and support can answer “why did I pay this?” without archaeology. Monitor redemption velocity, code-sharing sites, bot-like redemption patterns, employee-code leakage, and refund-and-keep-discount abuse; alert on spikes the same day, not at month-end. Close the loop monthly: which promos created incremental margin, which trained customers to wait for sales, and what elasticity estimates need re-measuring.

05 Code sketch

A guarded price calculation: elasticity is context, the promo is an object, the floor always wins.

from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import datetime, timezone

@dataclass
class Promo:
    percent_off: float          # e.g. 0.20 for 20% off
    max_redemptions: int
    redeemed: int = 0
    expires_at: datetime | None = None
    first_time_only: bool = False

def elasticity(q1, q2, p1, p2) -> float:  # midpoint / arc method
    dq = (q2 - q1) / ((q1 + q2) / 2)
    dp = (p2 - p1) / ((p1 + p2) / 2)
    return dq / dp if dp != 0 else 0.0

def final_price(list_price: float, promo: Promo | None,
                  cost_floor: float, is_first: bool) -> float:
    price = list_price
    if promo and promo.redeemed < promo.max_redemptions:
        fresh = promo.expires_at is None or promo.expires_at > datetime.now(timezone.utc)
        eligible = fresh and (is_first or not promo.first_time_only)
        if eligible:
            price = list_price * (1 - promo.percent_off)
    return round(max(price, cost_floor), 2)  # floor always wins

06 Failure modes

Five ways pricing and promotions fail — each with a one-line fix.

1

One elasticity for everythingFix: estimate elasticity per segment, channel, and price interval, stamp it with the observed price range, and re-measure after any large move.

A single “our elasticity is −1.8” hides that demand is inelastic at today’s price for loyal customers and highly elastic for new visitors — so the same 20% cut is wasteful for one group and insufficient for the other, and extrapolating it to a 50% cut invents precision you never measured.

2

Stacking and loophole discountsFix: define stacking and ordering in code, cap the combined discount, enforce the floor at checkout, and make single-use redemption atomic.

Two codes, a sale price, and loyalty points combine into 90% off; a shared “single-use” code is redeemed in parallel sessions before the counter updates. The margin loss is discovered by finance weeks later, one order at a time.

3

Judging a promo by conversion aloneFix: require a holdout and judge on incremental contribution margin, including full-price cannibalisation and post-promo repeat rate.

The dashboard glows green — conversion +30% — while most redemptions came from people who would have bought anyway, and repeat purchases at full price fall because customers learned to wait for the next code. The promo rented demand and trained the wrong behaviour.

4

Unguarded automation: below-cost or runaway pricesFix: enforce floor, ceiling, and speed limits in the pricing service, refuse stale inputs, and keep a tested kill switch with a named owner.

A repricing rule follows a bad competitor feed to $1, or a demand spike surges a necessity’s price overnight. The rule did exactly what it was told — nobody told it where to stop — and the correction costs more in refunds and trust than the automation ever saved.

5

Leaked, immortal, or untraceable codesFix: give every code an expiry, a redemption cap, and per-customer limits; store code + coupon + amounts on the order; alert on redemption-velocity spikes.

An influencer code with no cap ends up on coupon sites, a “temporary” code from last year still works, and nobody can say how much any of it cost because orders never recorded which discount was applied. Promotions you cannot trace you cannot stop.

Audit this proposed pricing/promotion change before launch: [paste the proposal, current prices, costs, and any automation involved]. Give me: (1) a guardrail check — floor (cost + fees + minimum margin), ceiling, max change per period, mid-checkout price stability, approval level required — with pass/fail on each, (2) the experiment plan: holdout design, primary metric (incremental contribution margin), cannibalisation and pull-forward measures, and the kill criteria, (3) the failure rehearsal: bad feed, stale data, leaked code, stacking bug — detection signal and kill-switch step for each, and (4) the launch checklist in dependency order, ending with what we will review at day 7 and day 30.

10 Flashcards

Click a card (or focus it and press Enter / Space) to flip it.

Price elasticity of demand
E = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price. Always negative in sign (price up, quantity down); reported as an absolute value. It measures responsiveness, not slope.
Elastic vs inelastic
|E| > 1 = elastic: quantity moves more than price, so a cut can raise revenue and a rise lowers it. |E| < 1 = inelastic: quantity moves less, so a rise raises revenue. |E| = 1 = unitary: revenue unchanged.
Midpoint (arc) elasticity
Percent changes computed against the average of the two values, not the starting value. The same interval then yields the same elasticity whether price increased or decreased.
Elasticity is not slope
Slope is constant along a straight-line demand curve; elasticity is not — it is high at high prices / low quantities and low at low prices / high quantities, because it works in percentages.
Cross-price elasticity
% change in quantity of good A / % change in price of good B. Positive = substitutes (discounting B steals A’s sales), negative = complements, zero = unrelated. Use it to spot cannibalisation.
Coupon vs promotion code
Stripe’s split: the coupon is the backend discount object (percent_off / amount_off, duration); the promotion code is the customer-facing string on top of it, carrying who/when/how-often restrictions. Many codes can point to one coupon.
Price floor
The lowest allowed final price: unit cost + channel/payment fees + minimum acceptable margin, plus any contractual or MAP constraints. Enforced at checkout after all discounts stack — the floor always wins.
Incrementality / holdout
A promo’s real value is what happened versus a comparable group that got no promo: incremental margin, not raw conversion. Redemptions by people who would have paid full price are cannibalisation — a cost.
Pricing guardrails (the five)
Floor, ceiling, speed limit (max change per period, no mid-checkout changes), approval ladder (auto inside the band, review at the edges, escalate outside), and a kill switch a named owner can pull in minutes.
Pull-forward & discount training
Two hidden promo costs: pull-forward borrows future sales into the promo window (next month dips), and repeated discounting trains customers to never pay list price — both show up only if you measure after the promo ends.

11 Reach for this when…

You are about to change a price on instinct — someone says “raise it 10%” or “run 20% off.” Estimate elasticity for that segment and interval first, run the revenue and margin math for elastic vs inelastic, and let the number pick the direction.
A promotion is being launched from a spreadsheet or a chat message — no object, no caps, no expiry. Stop and encode it properly: eligibility, stacking, floor, redemption limits, order-level audit fields, and a holdout, before the first code goes out.
Anything automated can change prices — a repricer, a dynamic-pricing model, or an AI agent with pricing tools. The automation proposes; floors, ceilings, speed limits, approvals, stale-data rejection, and the kill switch decide what customers ever see.
