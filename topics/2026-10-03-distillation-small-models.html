Distillation: small models from big ones

Don't pay frontier prices for production traffic — copy the behavior instead. Knowledge distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher’s outputs: the teacher’s softened probability distributions (“dark knowledge”) reveal similarity structure that hard labels throw away, so the student learns more from every example. A 10×–3,000× smaller model keeps most of the teacher’s quality on your task — this is the compression trick behind the cheap, fast small models that actually get deployed.

02 The one-idea version

A big model’s behavior is a function you can copy cheaper than you can run it. The teacher never hands over its weights — it hands over its softened output distributions, which say not just “the answer is 7” but “it’s 90% a 7, 8% a 9, and almost never a 1.” Training a small student against that distribution transfers the teacher’s similarity structure, so each example teaches what many hard-label examples would. The student won’t exceed the teacher, but on a well-defined task it gets close — at a fraction of the cost, latency, and memory.

04 How it works

Six mechanics, each one a decision you'd make in production.

M1

Pick white-box or black-box

White-box: you have the teacher's weights — match its softened logits with KL divergence (plus hidden states if architectures align). Black-box: you only have API access — generate teacher outputs and train the student on them with ordinary supervised loss. Most production small models today are built black-box.

M2

Mine the dark knowledge

Hard labels say “this is a 7.” The teacher's soft distribution says “90% a 7, 8% a 9, 0.1% a 1” — that's information about similarity structure. Train the student against the softened distribution and one example teaches what many hard-label examples would.

M3

Set temperature and alpha deliberately

Soften the teacher's logits with temperature T (start 2–5) and blend the losses: loss = α·T²·KL(student_T, teacher_T) + (1−α)·CE(student, labels), α ≈ 0.5–0.7. The T² factor keeps the KL term on the same scale as the cross-entropy term as T changes — Hinton's original formula.

M4

For LLMs, distill at the sequence level

Per-token soft targets are impractical without the teacher's logits and awkward for generation. Instead, generate complete teacher outputs and train the student on those sequences (Kim & Rush, 2016). Reasoning traces, structured formats, and multi-turn dialogues distill especially well this way.

M5

Prune the teacher, then distill

The strongest small models start life as a pruned teacher: width-prune (drop neurons/heads/channels) rather than depth-prune (drop layers) — accuracy survives width pruning better. Then retrain with distillation exclusively. If depth was heavily cut, distill logits + intermediate states + embeddings; if depth is mostly intact, logit-only is enough (Minitron/NVIDIA guidance).

M6

Evaluate the student like a deployment

Grade the student on the same benchmarks and real workloads as the teacher — the student is bounded by the teacher's quality — and on deployment metrics: latency, throughput, memory, cost per 1k requests. Distillation is a production optimization; judge it by production numbers.

05 Code sketch

Hinton's loss in 22 lines: teacher in eval mode, student chases soft targets plus hard labels.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def distill_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=3.0, alpha=0.6):
    # soft targets: temperature reveals the dark knowledge
    p_teacher = F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1)
    log_p_student = F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1)
    kd = F.kl_div(log_p_student, p_teacher, reduction="batchmean") * T**2
    # hard labels keep the student grounded in ground truth
    ce = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)
    return alpha * kd + (1 - alpha) * ce

teacher.eval()  # teacher guides; it never learns
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.no_grad():
        t_logits = teacher(x)          # forward once, no gradients
    s_logits = student(x)

06 Failure modes

Named pitfalls, each with a one-line fix.

F1

Student learns the style, not the substance — the imitation gap — The student apes the teacher's tone and formatting but falls short on reasoning and factual correctness.Fix: Distill on narrow, well-specified tasks; never assume broad reasoning transfers; re-run the teacher's full benchmark suite on the student, not just vibes.

F2

Temperature set blindly — T=1 makes soft targets nearly as hard as labels (no dark knowledge learned); T too high flattens everything into noise.Fix: Sweep T in 2–5 and watch validation; pair it with the α blend so ground truth keeps a vote.

F3

Distilling from a stale or shifted teacher — The teacher's guidance is only as good as its fit to your data distribution.Fix: Lightly fine-tune the teacher on your target data first, then distill; refresh the student when the teacher updates.

F4

Judging by accuracy only — The whole point of distillation is deployment — latency, memory, throughput, cost per 1k requests.Fix: Score the student on the teacher's task benchmarks AND the deployment metrics; ship on the production numbers.

F5

Assuming alignment transfers — Small students are weaker at instruction following and safety — distilled models can shed the teacher's refusal behavior and guardrail robustness.Fix: Re-run your safety and prompt-injection evals on the student itself, not just on the teacher.

10 Flashcards

Click or press Enter/Space to flip each card.

Dark knowledge
The extra information in the teacher's full output distribution (e.g. “7” is closer to “9” than to “1”) that hard one-hot labels discard. This is what the student actually learns from.
Soft targets
The teacher's probability distribution, softened by temperature. Richer training signal per example than a hard label: the student learns the teacher's ranking of wrong answers too.
Temperature T
The softmax divisor in the distillation loss. Higher T flattens the distribution, exposing more dark knowledge; T=1 gives ordinary predictions. Typical range 2–5.
Alpha (α)
The weight balancing the distillation loss (match the teacher) against the hard-label loss (match the ground truth). α ≈ 0.5–0.7 is a common starting point.
The T² factor
Soft-target gradients shrink as 1/T², so the KL term is scaled by T² to stay comparable to the cross-entropy term as temperature changes. It's in Hinton's 2015 formula.
Black-box distillation
Distilling with only API access to the teacher: generate teacher outputs for your inputs and train the student on them with ordinary supervised loss. No weights or logits needed.
Sequence-level distillation
For LLMs: train the student on whole generations produced by the teacher, not per-token soft labels. The form that powers most modern small LLMs (Kim & Rush, 2016).
Prune-then-distill
Start the student by pruning the teacher itself — width pruning beats depth pruning for accuracy — then retrain with distillation exclusively (NVIDIA/Minitron guidance).
The imitation gap
Students mimic the teacher's style easily but inherit broad reasoning and factual depth poorly. Distillation shines on narrow, well-specified tasks.

11 Reach for this when…

Your per-request cost is dominated by a frontier model on a narrow, well-defined task — distillation routinely recovers most of the quality at a fraction of the cost and latency.
You need the model on-device, on a single GPU, or under a latency SLO the teacher can't meet — a distilled student is how frontier capability fits in a phone or a 100ms budget.
You're serving the same kind of request thousands of times a day — distill the teacher's answers once, then serve the cheap student forever; the training cost amortizes in days.
