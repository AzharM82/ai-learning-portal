Long-context engineering: million-token windows, lost-in-the-middle, and compaction

A window that holds a million tokens sounds like the end of context problems. It is not. Accuracy decays as input grows, the middle of a long input is used worst, and every extra token is paid for in attention, money, and latency on every single turn. Long-context engineering is the discipline of spending a big window like a small budget.

02 The one-idea version

The window is capacity; attention is the budget — and the budget shrinks as you spend it. A transformer lets every token attend to every other token, so a longer input means n² pairwise relationships competing for the same attention budget (Anthropic’s framing). The result is a gradient, not a cliff: recall and reasoning get less precise as tokens accumulate, and information in the middle is used worst of all. Engineering long context therefore means curating, ordering, and shrinking what is in the window — not celebrating how much fits.

04 How it works

Seven mechanics: capacity vs effectiveness, position, rot, selection, clearing and compaction, offloading, and honest evaluation.

1

Separate the advertised window from the effective window

Count everything against the window: system prompt, tool definitions, every message, tool results, documents, images, and the output you reserve — cached tokens still occupy it, they are just cheaper. Google’s Gemini guide puts 1M tokens at about 50,000 lines of code or eight average novels, which is real capacity. But capacity is a ceiling, not a quality guarantee: your effective window is the length at which your task still passes your evals, and it is almost always shorter. Budget to the effective window, and leave headroom for the answer.

2

Order for the U-curve: edges carry the load

Liu et al. (“Lost in the Middle”) moved one answer-bearing document through a multi-document context and found a U-shaped curve: best at the very beginning (primacy) and end (recency), significantly worse in the middle — in one setting GPT-3.5-Turbo with the answer mid-context scored below its no-documents baseline. So: put instructions and the most relevant evidence first, put the question last (Google’s long-context FAQ recommends exactly this), and when the query is known up front, repeat it after the documents — the paper’s query-aware contextualization fixed its synthetic retrieval task. Never bury the one clause, figure, or file the answer depends on in the middle third.

3

Expect context rot: a gradient, not a cliff

Chroma’s Context Rot study held task complexity constant, varied only input length, and tested 18 frontier models: performance degraded in every model, non-uniformly, well before the advertised limit — and focused prompts of ~300 tokens beat full ~113K-token histories on its conversation task. Anthropic gives the mechanism and the name: attention is a finite budget stretched over n² token pairs, and models are trained mostly on shorter sequences, so “as token count grows, accuracy and recall degrade.” Plan for gradual decay from the first tens of thousands of tokens, not a wall at 1M.

4

Select the smallest high-signal set — retrieve, don’t stuff

Anthropic’s guiding principle is the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximizes the chance of the outcome. In practice that means retrieval or just-in-time loading (keep lightweight identifiers — file paths, queries, links — and load data with tools at runtime, as Claude Code does with glob/grep) instead of pre-loading everything, and a hybrid when latency matters: a small curated core up front, exploration on demand. Long context does not kill RAG; it changes its job from “fit anything at all” to “keep distractors out” — Chroma found even a single similar-but-wrong distractor measurably hurts.

5

Clear first, then compact — deliberately and early

For long agent runs, pull the levers in cost order. Clearing is the lightest touch: drop raw tool results once they have been processed (the model rarely needs the full JSON blob again). Compaction is next: summarize a conversation nearing the limit and restart the window from that summary, as Claude Code does — preserving architectural decisions, unresolved bugs, and implementation details plus the most recently used files, discarding redundant output. Tune compaction recall-first (capture everything relevant), then precision (cut the superfluous), and compact at a natural breakpoint on your schedule — not in a panic at 99% full, when the summary itself must be generated by a rotted context.

6

Offload to notes and isolate in sub-agents

What must survive compaction should not live only in the window. Structured note-taking: the agent writes decisions, plans, and state to a file or memory store outside the context and reads it back on demand — Anthropic’s research agent saves its plan to memory precisely because context beyond the limit gets truncated. Isolation: give sub-agents clean, focused windows for deep search or exploration and let only a distilled result return to the lead agent, so detailed search context never pollutes the main thread. Compaction keeps one long conversation coherent; notes excel at milestone-driven work; isolation excels at parallel exploration — pick per task shape, and combine them.

7

Evaluate at length, at position, against distractors

Needle-in-a-haystack is necessary and nowhere near sufficient: Chroma notes it is mostly lexical matching, and scores collapse when the needle needs semantic inference, when similar distractors appear, or when the task requires reasoning rather than retrieval. Build evals that mirror production: your real documents at your real token lengths, the answer placed at start / middle / end, with realistic distractors, and a focused-context baseline to compare against. If the focused baseline wins — as it did in Chroma’s conversation tests — your context assembly, not the model, is the bug. Re-run when you change models: effective length is a property of a model and a task, not a spec-sheet number.

05 Code sketch

A small Python context assembler: budget check, edge-first ordering, and a compaction trigger that fires before rot, not after overflow.

# Assemble long context: edges first, budget enforced, compact early
BUDGET = 180_000          # effective window from evals, not the 1M spec
COMPACT_AT = 0.70         # compact at 70% full, while summaries are still good

def order_docs(docs: list[dict]) -> list[dict]:
    # docs pre-ranked by relevance: best first AND last (U-curve)
    if len(docs) < 3: return docs
    return [docs[0]] + docs[2:] + [docs[1]]

def assemble(instructions: str, docs, history, question: str,
             count_tokens) -> dict:
    parts = {"system": instructions,
             "docs": [d["text"] for d in order_docs(docs)],
             "history": history, "question": question}  # question last
    total = sum(count_tokens(str(v)) for v in parts.values())
    if total > BUDGET * COMPACT_AT:
        parts["history"] = summarize(history, keep=[
            "decisions", "open_bugs", "current_plan"])  # recall-first
        parts["docs"] = parts["docs"][:5]  # drop distractors, keep edges
    total = sum(count_tokens(str(v)) for v in parts.values())
    assert total < BUDGET, "still over effective budget: retrieve less"
    return parts

06 Failure modes

Five ways long-context features go wrong in production — and the one-line fix for each.

1

Stuffing because it fitsFix: assemble the smallest high-signal set that could answer, retrieve the rest just-in-time, and compare against a focused-context baseline in evals.

“The repo fits in 1M tokens, so send the repo.” Every turn now pays full price and full latency, distractors included, while accuracy quietly slides down the rot gradient. Fitting was never the constraint that mattered — attention was.

2

Burying the needle in the middleFix: rank evidence, place the most relevant items first and last, put the question at the end, and test accuracy with the answer in each position.

Documents concatenated in retrieval or chronological order put the decisive passage at position 11 of 20 — exactly where Liu et al. measured the deepest dip, sometimes below answering with no documents at all. The model did not lack the information; the layout hid it.

3

Declaring victory on needle-in-a-haystackFix: evaluate semantic retrieval, multi-needle and reasoning tasks, realistic distractors, and full-length production-shaped prompts — not lexical needles alone.

Near-perfect NIAH scores get read as “long context is solved.” Chroma showed the same models degrading on tasks only slightly harder — a needle that must be inferred rather than matched, or one distractor that merely resembles the answer. Your users’ questions look like the second set, not the first.

4

Compacting too late, or compacting away the decisionsFix: compact early at a set threshold, use a recall-first summary schema (decisions, open issues, plan, key files), and keep a notes file outside the window as the source of truth.

Auto-compaction fires at 95%+ from an already-degraded context, and its summary drops the one architectural decision made three hours ago. The agent continues fluently — and re-litigates, re-breaks, or contradicts work it can no longer see. Lossy and late is the worst cell in the table.

5

Re-sending a giant static context on every turnFix: cache the stable prefix (system prompt, reference corpus), clear processed tool results, and keep per-turn additions small — caching cuts price, it does not excuse bloat.

A support agent re-sends 400K tokens of history and documents for a one-line follow-up, sixty times a day. Latency and cost scale with the window you actually send, not the question you actually asked — and the follow-up’s answer still rots in the pile.

10 Flashcards

Click a card (or focus it and press Enter / Space) to flip it.

Advertised vs effective window
Advertised: the maximum tokens a model accepts (now commonly 1M). Effective: the length at which your task still passes your evals. The effective window is shorter, task-dependent, and the only one worth budgeting to.
Lost in the middle
Liu et al.’s finding that models use information at the start and end of a long context best and information in the middle worst — a U-shaped accuracy curve across multi-document QA and key-value retrieval.
Context rot
The gradual degradation of accuracy and recall as input tokens grow, documented by Chroma across 18 models and named in Anthropic’s guidance. A gradient that starts well before the window fills — not a cliff at the limit.
Attention budget
Anthropic’s metaphor for finite attention: every token attends to every other token (n² pairs), so each added token spends some of a fixed budget. Curation is how you stop low-signal tokens from taxing high-signal ones.
Compaction
Summarizing a conversation nearing the limit and restarting the window from that summary — preserving decisions, open issues, and key details while discarding redundant output. Tune recall first, then precision; trigger it early, at a breakpoint.
Tool-result clearing
The lightest form of compaction: delete raw tool outputs from the window once they have been processed, keeping the fact of the call and any distilled result. Targets the fastest-growing, lowest-signal tokens in an agent loop first.
Structured note-taking
The agent writes plans, decisions, and state to storage outside the context window and reads them back on demand. It is what survives compaction and truncation — memory as a file, not as hope.
Just-in-time retrieval
Keep lightweight identifiers (paths, queries, links) in context and load the actual data with tools at runtime, discovering it progressively. The alternative to pre-loading everything — and to stale pre-built indexes.
Distractor
Context that resembles the answer but is not the answer. Chroma found even one distractor measurably lowers accuracy, more so at longer lengths — which is why selecting context out is as important as retrieving it in.

11 Reach for this when…

A design starts with “just put it all in the window” — a whole repo, a full document set, an entire chat history. That sentence is your cue to ask for the effective window, the ordering, and the focused-baseline eval before anyone builds it.
An agent gets worse the longer it runs — forgetting early decisions, contradicting itself, drowning in tool output. That is rot plus growth: clear tool results, compact on a threshold, and move the plan into notes outside the window.
A vendor claim rests on a needle test — near-perfect recall at 1M tokens is a retrieval floor, not a reasoning guarantee. Reach for position-and-distractor evals at your real length before you bet a feature on the number.
