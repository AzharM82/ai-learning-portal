Preference tuning: RLHF, DPO, and teaching models what "good" means

Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model what an answer looks like. Preference tuning teaches it which of two plausible answers people actually prefer — more helpful, more honest, less harmful. Classic RLHF turns those comparisons into a reward model and optimizes against it with PPO; DPO skips both and optimizes the same objective directly from chosen / rejected pairs.

02 The one-idea version

"Good" is easier to recognize than to demonstrate — so train on comparisons, not just examples. A labeler who cannot write the perfect summary can still reliably pick the better of two. Preference tuning turns thousands of those picks into a training signal: in RLHF a reward model learns to predict the pick and PPO chases its score under a KL leash; in DPO the model itself is the implicit reward model, and a simple classification loss widens the margin between chosen and rejected, relative to a frozen reference.

03 The picture: two routes from the same pairs

Same starting point, same data, same KL constraint. RLHF builds a judge and then runs a reinforcement-learning loop; DPO folds the judge into the loss.

04 How it works

Six mechanics: start from SFT, collect pairs, pick your route, control drift, and prove it on preferences you did not train on.

1

Start from a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model — never from the raw base

Preference tuning is a second stage. First fine-tune on demonstrations so the model already produces assistant-shaped answers in-distribution. The InstructGPT recipe (Ouyang et al.) is explicit: labeler demonstrations fine-tune GPT-3, then rankings are collected from that SFT model’s outputs. Hugging Face TRL says the same for DPO: train the SFT model first so the preference data is in-distribution for the algorithm. Pairs collected from a different model’s style teach style transfer and preference at once — badly.

2

Collect comparisons: (prompt, chosen, rejected), not absolute scores

Show labelers several completions for one prompt and have them rank or pick the best. Humans are far more consistent at “A is better than B” than at “A is a 7.3.” Model the pick with Bradley–Terry: P(chosen ≻ rejected) = σ(r(chosen) − r(rejected)). Record the rubric (helpful, honest, harmless — and what wins a tie), labeler agreement, and prompt source. Your preference dataset is your definition of good; if it is noisy, narrow, or all from one demographic, the tuned model faithfully learns exactly that.

3

Classic RLHF: distill the picks into a reward model

Train a reward model r(x, y) — usually the SFT model with a scalar head — to assign higher scores to chosen than rejected under the Bradley–Terry loss. Now you have a fast, automatic stand-in for the labelers: it can score millions of fresh generations no human will ever see. That is both the power and the danger. The reward model is a proxy: it generalizes imperfectly, and everything downstream will exploit wherever the proxy differs from real human judgment.

4

Optimize the policy against the reward — with PPO and a KL leash

Run reinforcement learning (classically PPO): sample completions from the current policy, score them with the reward model, and update the policy to earn more reward — while paying a KL penalty for drifting from the frozen SFT reference. The KL term is not decoration: without it the policy quickly finds degenerate, reward-model-pleasing text. Even with it, budget for instability: RLHF juggles policy, reference, reward, and often a value model, needs on-policy sampling throughout, and is notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters. InstructGPT’s payoff for this machinery was historic — the 1.3B model’s outputs were preferred to the 175B GPT-3’s.

5

DPO: skip the reward model and the RL loop — optimize the pairs directly

Rafailov et al. showed the KL-constrained reward-maximization problem has a closed-form optimum, so the reward can be reparameterized as r(x,y) = β · log[π(y|x) / πref(y|x)] plus a prompt-only constant that cancels in pairs. Substitute that into Bradley–Terry and RLHF becomes a binary-classification loss on the preference pairs themselves: −log σ(β · [(log-ratio for chosen) − (log-ratio for rejected)]). No separate reward model, no sampling during training, no PPO. In practice with TRL’s DPOTrainer, each example is just prompt / chosen / rejected, and the trainer in practice often lowers the rejected likelihood more than it raises the chosen one — watch both log-probabilities, not just the margin.

6

Tune beta, iterate the data, and evaluate on held-out human preference

beta controls how strongly preferences pull the policy away from the reference: TRL documents typical values around 0.1 to 0.5, with 0.1 the common start. Too small and the model barely moves (or, at the limit, ignores the reference); too aggressive a run and you get degeneration and length blow-up. Preference tuning is rarely one-and-done: evaluate, mine the new model’s failures, collect fresh pairs on its outputs (iterative / online variants), and re-train. Judge success by win-rate against the SFT model on held-out prompts scored by humans or a judge that did not produce the training reward — never by the training reward itself, which the policy was literally optimized to game.

05 Code sketch

A minimal DPO run with TRL: SFT model in, preference pairs in the documented format, beta as the drift control.

# pip install trl transformers datasets peft
from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer

# Each row: {"prompt": ..., "chosen": ..., "rejected": ...}
# chosen / rejected are two completions for the SAME prompt,
# ranked by humans (or a documented AI judge = RLAIF).
train_ds = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="train_prefs")

cfg = DPOConfig(
    beta=0.1,                 # KL strength: higher = stay closer to ref
    learning_rate=5e-7,       # full model; ~5e-6 to 1e-5 with LoRA
    per_device_train_batch_size=4,
    num_train_epochs=1,       # preference data overfits fast
    logging_steps=10,         # watch rewards/margins + accuracies
    output_dir="out/dpo-v1",
)

trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model="my-org/my-sft-model",   # the SFT checkpoint, NOT the base
    args=cfg,
    train_dataset=train_ds,
    # ref model: omitted -> trainer uses a frozen copy of the SFT model;
    # with PEFT, the adapter-off base serves as the reference.
)
trainer.train()
trainer.save_model("out/dpo-v1/final")
# Gate on held-out win-rate vs the SFT model before shipping.

06 Failure modes

Five ways preference tuning makes a model worse while the training metrics look great — and the one-line fix for each.

1

Reward hacking and over-optimizationFix: keep the KL / beta leash on, cap epochs, and evaluate with held-out humans — a rising training reward with a flat or falling human win-rate is the hacking signature.

The policy finds what the reward model (or the DPO margin) rewards rather than what people want: longer, more confident, more flattering answers that score well and help less. Goodhart’s law with gradients. In DPO the same pressure appears as verbosity and sycophancy learned straight from biased pairs.

2

Garbage preferences in, faithful garbage outFix: write the ranking rubric, measure inter-labeler agreement, audit a sample of pairs by hand, and drop or relabel systematic disagreements before training.

Labelers reward length, formatting, or agreeableness because the rubric never said not to; two labeler pools disagree and their labels cancel into noise; AI-generated preferences (RLAIF) quietly encode the judge model’s own biases. The tuned model does not average this out — it amplifies whatever pattern is most learnable in the pairs.

3

DPO margin grows while chosen answers get worseFix: log chosen and rejected log-probabilities separately; if both are falling, add an SFT anchor loss on chosen (as in RPO / DPO+SFT mixes) or lower the learning rate and beta pressure.

The DPO loss only cares about the gap between chosen and rejected relative to the reference. The gap can widen by pushing rejected down — and chosen down with it, just slower (likelihood displacement). Outputs drift off-distribution even as rewards/margins and accuracy climb. TRL’s own docs note DPO in practice often works mainly by suppressing the rejected completions.

4

Beta / KL mis-set: no learning, or degenerationFix: start at beta 0.1, run a small sweep, and watch KL / reward margin together — pick the smallest drift that clears your held-out win-rate bar.

Too much constraint and preference training is a no-op you ship with confidence; too little and the policy chases the preference signal into repetitive, overlong, or strangely formatted text that no pair ever endorsed. The symptom is misdiagnosed as a data problem when it is a one-knob problem.

5

Grading yourself with the training judgeFix: freeze a held-out preference eval set before training, score it with independent humans or a different judge, and include capability regressions (not just win-rate) in the ship gate.

The team reports “reward up 40%” or win-rate judged by the same model that generated the RLAIF labels. Of course it agrees with itself. Meanwhile general capability quietly regressed — the alignment tax nobody measured. InstructGPT checked public NLP benchmarks precisely to keep this honest.

10 Flashcards

Click a card (or focus it and press Enter / Space) to flip it.

Preference pair
One training example for preference tuning: a prompt plus two completions, one chosen and one rejected by a human or documented AI judge. The dataset of pairs is the operational definition of "good".
Bradley–Terry model
The probability model behind both RLHF and DPO: P(chosen beats rejected) = sigmoid(r(chosen) − r(rejected)). Only reward differences matter, which is why prompt-only constants cancel in DPO.
Reward model
In classic RLHF, a model trained on human rankings to output a scalar score predicting human preference. It lets PPO score fresh generations at scale — and it is a proxy the policy will learn to game.
KL penalty / leash
A term in the RLHF objective charging the policy for diverging from the frozen SFT reference. It trades raw reward for staying near known-good behavior, limiting reward hacking and degeneration.
DPO loss
−log sigmoid( beta × [(log π(chosen)/π_ref(chosen)) − (log π(rejected)/π_ref(rejected))] ). A classification loss on pairs that optimizes the same KL-constrained objective as RLHF, with no reward model or RL loop.
Implicit reward
DPO's insight: a policy defines a reward, r(x,y) = beta · log[π(y|x) / π_ref(y|x)] (up to a prompt constant). Hence "your language model is secretly a reward model".
Beta (β)
DPO's drift-control knob, roughly the inverse temperature on the implicit reward. Typical starts around 0.1 (TRL documents ~0.1–0.5). Too weak: little learning or runaway drift in effect; too strong a pull: over-optimization on the pairs.
Reward hacking
The policy exploits gaps between the proxy reward and real human judgment — verbosity, confidence, sycophancy — so training reward rises while true quality stalls or falls. Detect it with held-out human win-rate, never the training reward.
RLAIF
Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback: preferences come from an AI judge (often guided by written principles, as in Constitutional AI) instead of humans. Cheaper and scalable, but it encodes the judge model's biases — audit it like a labeler pool.

11 Reach for this when…

Your SFT model is plausible but not preferred — answers are well-formed yet verbose, hedging, unhelpful, or occasionally unsafe, and you can say which of two outputs is better more easily than you can write a perfect one. That comparative gap is exactly preference data.
You need assistant behavior, not just task skill — helpfulness, honesty, refusal boundaries, tone. These are judgment calls without a single correct string, so demonstrations under-specify them and comparisons carry the signal.
You have (or can collect) ranked outputs from your real distribution — production logs, labeler rankings, or audited AI-judge pairs on your own prompts. Start with DPO for its simplicity; reach for full RLHF only when you need online exploration beyond a fixed pair dataset and can afford its instability.
