Marketplace engineering: supply, demand, matching, and trust

A marketplace owns no inventory. Its product is a successful transaction between strangers: enough supply and demand in the same narrow place, matched correctly, paid safely, and trusted enough to repeat.

02 The one-idea version

Liquidity is the product. A marketplace works only when, in a specific category and place and time window, a buyer reliably finds acceptable supply and a seller reliably finds paying demand. Everything else — search, ranking, payments, reviews — exists to raise that match rate without breaking trust. Start narrow enough that matches actually happen, prove the loop by hand if you must, then let software scale a loop that already works.

03 The picture: supply, matching, demand, trust

Two sides, one matching engine, and a trust layer underneath every step.

04 How it works

Seven mechanics: win liquidity narrowly, match correctly, move money exactly once, and make trust structural.

1

Define the atomic network and seed the harder side first

Pick the smallest unit where liquidity can be real: one city, one category, one use case. That is your atomic network. Recruit the constrained side — usually supply — by hand before spending on demand: concierge matching, manual onboarding, even doing the service yourself. Sharetribe's concierge model is explicit: learn demand by talking to it, match it to your best supply personally, and use those first successful transactions as proof. A thin marketplace across ten cities loses to a dense one in one zip code, every time.

2

Model the transaction as a state machine, with a ledger and a snapshot

Listings, requests, and transactions are the core entities. Give the transaction named states — pending → confirmed → in_progress → completed, with disputed and cancelled branches — and rules for who can trigger each transition. Snapshot price, commission, and listing terms at purchase time, because sellers edit listings later and the receipt must not change. Record money as a double-entry ledger in integer minor units: every charge, platform fee, refund, and payout is its own auditable row, never a float overwritten on the order.

3

Gate eligibility before you rank anything

Hard constraints come first and are deterministic: category/skill match, service area, time window with capacity > 0, verified and compliant status, budget ceiling, blocklists. An unavailable or unqualified supplier must never appear, however high they would score. Implement gates as composable, logged functions and store which gate failed for each excluded supplier — that log is your match debugger, your supplier coaching tool, and your explanation when someone asks why they were not shown.

4

Rank for acceptance, with fairness and exploration built in

Rank only the eligible set, with a transparent weighted score you can ship this week: proximity/travel time, price fit, skill relevance, and performance (acceptance rate, on-time rate, response latency, cancellation rate). Then add policy: cap exposure for over-shown suppliers, boost verified newcomers, and run small epsilon exploration so new supply can earn a track record. Optimise for acceptance and completed transactions, not clicks — a click that ends in a decline destroys trust on both sides. Version the ranker, log feature contributions, and A/B test against fill rate and cancellations.

5

Build the trust stack in layers, visible at the decision moment

Strangers transact when risk feels capped. Layer: verified identity (KYC/KYB for sellers), two-sided reviews only after a completed transaction, secure on-platform payments (funds held until delivery milestones pass), guarantees/insurance for the exposed side, clear cancellation and dispute rules, and moderation of listings and messages. Each layer removes a specific fear. Put the signals where the decision happens — on the listing and at checkout — not buried in a help centre, and keep contact and payment on-platform so disputes have a paper trail.

6

Split payments through connected accounts; treat webhooks as truth

Marketplace money is not a normal checkout: one buyer payment must split into seller payout minus platform fee, survive refunds and chargebacks, and pay out to a verified bank account. Stripe's marketplace guide models this with a connected account per seller, hosted onboarding for identity collection, explicit assignment of who collects fees and who bears losses, and an application fee for your commission. Choose the charge model (direct vs destination vs separate charges and transfers) by who should be merchant of record. Never trust a browser redirect for payment state — webhooks are the source of truth for payment, refund, dispute, and payout status, handled idempotently.

7

Measure liquidity health and defend against leakage

Track per atomic network, not globally: search-to-request rate, fill rate (requests that reach a completed transaction), time to first response and time to match, supplier utilisation, repeat rate on both sides, cancellation and dispute rates, and take-rate after refunds. Global averages hide dead segments. Finally, expect disintermediation: once matched, both sides are tempted to transact off-platform and skip the fee. Counter it with value only the platform provides — payment protection, guarantees, rebooking, records, and reputation that does not transfer — not just with bans.

05 Code sketch

Eligibility gates, a transparent ranker, and a ledger split for one transaction.

def eligible(s, req):
    # Hard gates first — fail fast, log the reason
    if not s["verified"]: return "unverified"
    if req["category"] not in s["categories"]: return "category"
    if s["capacity"] <= 0: return "no_capacity"
    if s["price"] > req["budget"]: return "over_budget"
    return None  # None = eligible

def score(s, req):
    perf = 0.6 * s["accept_rate"] + 0.4 * s["on_time_rate"]
    price_fit = 1 - abs(s["price"] - req["budget"]) / req["budget"]
    fair = max(0.0, 0.2 - s["recent_exposure"])  # damp over-exposed
    return round(0.35*perf + 0.25*price_fit + 0.25*s["relevance"] + fair, 3)

def rank(suppliers, req):
    pool = [s for s in suppliers if eligible(s, req) is None]
    return sorted(pool, key=lambda s: score(s, req), reverse=True)

def split_payment(amount_cents, take_bps=1200):
    # Ledger rows in integer cents — never overwrite a balance float
    fee = amount_cents * take_bps // 10000
    return [{"account": "buyer", "delta": -amount_cents},
            {"account": "platform", "delta": fee},
            {"account": "seller", "delta": amount_cents - fee}]

06 Failure modes

Five ways marketplaces quietly die — each with a one-line fix.

1

Launching broad and thin: no segment ever reaches liquidityFix: constrain to one atomic network, hit a target fill rate there, then replicate the playbook segment by segment.

Buyers who find nothing leave and do not return; sellers who get no work churn — anti-network effects compound faster than growth.

2

Ranking for clicks or revenue instead of completed matchesFix: make acceptance/completion the primary metric, with cancellations and disputes as guardrails on every ranking change.

Irrelevant or unavailable results train both sides that the platform wastes their time — the scarcest asset is attention from good supply.

3

Stale availability: "looks good" followed by "actually, we can't"Fix: treat availability as live state — event-driven updates, short reservation holds with expiry, and idempotent accept/decline.

Every false-positive match is a double rejection: the buyer is disappointed and the supplier is blamed for a system error.

4

Review inflation and gaming make reputation worthlessFix: reviews only after a verified completed transaction, two-sided and simultaneous reveal, and distributions shown — not just a 4.9 average.

When every seller is five stars, buyers stop believing the signal and fall back to price — exactly the race you built reputation to avoid.

5

Money handled as an afterthought: floats, manual payouts, no dispute pathFix: ledger-first money in minor units, connected accounts with verified onboarding, escrow/holds where risk demands it, and webhooks as payment truth.

Payment friction and one mishandled refund undo trust faster than any matching bug — and create regulatory exposure a retailer never faces.

10 Flashcards

Click a card (or focus it and press Enter / Space) to flip it.

Liquidity
The probability that a request in a given segment becomes a completed transaction quickly. The marketplace's core health metric — measured per atomic network, never just globally.
Atomic network
The smallest segment (city x category x use case) that can sustain its own supply-demand loop. Win one, prove the playbook, then replicate.
Cold start / chicken-and-egg
Neither side joins without the other. Solved by seeding the harder side first, constraining scope, subsidising that side, and hand-matching (concierge) until the loop self-sustains.
Eligibility gate vs ranking
Gates are hard, deterministic rules (verified, in area, capacity > 0, within budget) applied first. Ranking is a soft weighted score applied only to those who pass.
Fill rate
Share of demand requests that reach a completed transaction. Paired with time-to-match, it tells you whether a segment has liquidity or just traffic.
Trust stack
Layered mechanisms — verified identity, two-sided reviews, on-platform escrow payments, guarantees, dispute resolution — each capping a specific risk of transacting with a stranger.
Connected account / split payment
A payment account per seller so one buyer charge can be split: seller payout minus platform application fee, with refunds, disputes, and payouts handled per seller.
Take rate
The platform's commission as a share of transaction value (GMV). Sustainable only while the platform's matching, trust, and payment value exceeds what leaving would save.
Disintermediation (leakage)
Matched parties transacting off-platform to avoid the fee. Countered with on-platform-only value (protection, guarantees, reputation, records), not bans alone.
Double-entry ledger
Every money movement recorded as balanced rows in integer minor units — charge, fee, refund, payout — so balances are sums that can be audited and reversed, never overwritten floats.

11 Reach for this when…

Your product connects two independent sides who must transact, not just meet — design for liquidity, matching, split payments, and trust from day one; a directory with a checkout bolted on will leak.
Traffic is growing but completed transactions are not — instrument fill rate and time-to-match per segment, fix eligibility and availability freshness, and re-rank for acceptance before buying more traffic.
Users meet on your platform and pay each other elsewhere — audit what value disappears off-platform and rebuild the trust/payment offer until staying is the rational choice.
